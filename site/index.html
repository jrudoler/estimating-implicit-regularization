02 — ESTIMATING THE PENALTY

Reverse-engineering the regularization.

In Figure 1, we fixed the penalty and found the weights. We can exploit the same structure in reverse: fix the observed weights θ̂ and estimate the penalty. We hypothesize that θ̂ is stationary for the loss plus a regularizer from a chosen family R(θ; Λ), where Λ denotes the penalty parameters.

For differentiable objectives, stationarity requires the regularizer gradient to cancel the loss gradient. Both derivatives are taken with respect to the model weights θ and evaluated at the observed weights θ̂:

∇θL(θ^)+∇θR(θ^;Λ)=0

Exact cancellation may not be possible within the chosen family. We therefore fit Λ by minimizing the squared residual gradient. The weights θ̂ and training data stay fixed throughout this estimation; p is the number of model weights.

Λ^=arg minΛ1p‖∇θL(θ^)+∇θR(θ^;Λ)‖22

In the scalar example below, L(θ) = ½(θ − 2)² and R(θ; λ) = λθ² with λ ≥ 0. Their derivatives give the residual (θ̂ − 2) + 2λθ̂. For θ̂ ≠ 0, minimizing its square has the closed-form solution:

λ^=max(0,2−θ^2θ^)

Select “Estimate λ” to see the total minimum move toward the fixed observed weight. The loss gradient stays constant while the penalty gradient changes. The extended weight range includes observations that no nonnegative L₂ penalty can explain; at θ̂ = 0, the penalty gradient is zero for every λ, so its strength cannot be identified.

The inverse calculation and its limits

“Estimate λ” animates from the current guess to this exact least-squares solution; it is not a simulation of an iterative optimizer.

For 0 < θ̂ ≤ 2, the gradients can cancel exactly. Outside that interval, nonnegative ridge cannot explain the observed weight as an optimum: its best fit leaves a residual. At θ̂ = 0, every λ supplies the same zero penalty gradient, so λ is unidentifiable. A fitted coefficient is an explanation within the chosen family, not evidence that this mechanism generated the weights.

Stationarity and identifiability.

These quadratic bowls are convex, so a zero net gradient identifies the minimum. A neural network is more complicated: gradient cancellation alone establishes stationarity, not a global minimum. Several regularizers may explain the same endpoint.

With multiple penalty terms, we fit their coefficients together: −∇L ≈ λ₁∇R₁ + λ₂∇R₂ + … . Each weight contributes one equation. If two penalty gradients are nearly parallel, their separate coefficients can be difficult to identify even when their sum fits well.

03 — RECOVERING EXPLICIT REGULARIZATION

Recovering an L₂ penalty in a neural network.

First, we test recovery when the regularizer is known. Train a small network on noisy samples of a curve, using tanh by default or ReLU for piecewise-linear predictions. We train with an explicit L₂ penalty and withhold its coefficient from the estimator. The estimate uses only the weights and the gradient of the unregularized training loss. During training, this endpoint estimate may be inaccurate because the gradients have not yet reached a balance.

This is a simplified version of the paper’s explicit elastic-net recovery experiment. Here we estimate one L₂ coefficient in a small, single-input network. The paper jointly estimates smoothed L₁ and L₂ coefficients in a 200-unit ReLU network with 10 inputs, across repeated datasets and penalty settings.

Model, training, and estimator

We use mean squared error L = (1/n) Σ(fθ(xᵢ) − yᵢ)² and R = λ‖θ‖², penalizing all parameters, including biases (3 × width + 1 parameters). Full-batch gradient descent runs for up to 20,000 steps. The learning rate is 0.04 × 16 / width (0.04, 0.02, or 0.01) to keep wider networks stable. The data and initialization are reproducible for each seed.

λ̂ = max(0, −θᵀ∇L / (2‖θ‖²))

This is a nonnegative least-squares projection of −∇L onto 2θ. “Gradient explained” is 1 − ‖∇L + 2λ̂θ‖² / ‖∇L‖². It is a fit diagnostic, not predictive R². Near-zero weights make the coefficient unidentifiable; a near-zero loss gradient makes the explained fraction uninformative. We display a dash for unavailable diagnostics.

For ReLU, we use a zero derivative at a kink. Tanh uses its smooth derivative, 1 − tanh²(z). Finite training time and nonsmoothness can leave a residual even when the loss appears stable. This experiment demonstrates explicit-penalty recovery, not proof that an arbitrary implicit bias is ridge regularization.

ESTIMATING IMPLICIT REGULARIZATION

Testing an estimated regularizer.

Figure 4 checks whether gradient matching can recover an explicit penalty. For implicit regularization, we instead propose a penalty family to explain the effect of a training choice, such as early stopping or dropout. A good gradient fit is a local condition at the observed weights. It does not by itself identify the original mechanism or guarantee that retraining with the fitted penalty will reproduce the model.

The paper uses two forms of validation: recovering coefficients when the regularizer is known or predicted by theory, and replacing a training feature with the estimated explicit penalty to test whether it recovers the original weights. In linear regression, we recover the quadratic penalty predicted for early stopping by combining multiple training endpoints. A diagonal penalty fitted to a single endpoint can also reproduce the early-stopped weights, even though it differs from the theoretical penalty matrix.

Dropout illustrates the limits of a candidate family. In our MNIST experiments, a scalar L₂ penalty explains only a small fraction of the residual loss gradient. Replacing dropout with that fitted penalty also fails to reproduce the original test performance. The estimate describes a ridge-aligned component of the effect, but scalar ridge is an incomplete explanation of dropout.

Matching training updates.

The interactives above use the endpoint condition ∇L + ∇R ≈ 0. The paper also estimates regularization from deviations along training trajectories, where the weights are still moving. For discrete gradient descent, we compare updates with a continuous gradient-flow reference and recover the predicted implicit gradient-regularization coefficient. This uses update deviations rather than assuming that each intermediate iterate is stationary.
