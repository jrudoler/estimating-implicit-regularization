Estimating implicit regularization in deep learning

Information about implicit regularization is embedded in the dynamics of learning. We use gradients of the loss and a proposed regularizer to estimate its unknown parameters.

Joseph H. Rudoler · Kevin Tan · Giles Hooker · Konrad P. Kording

University of Pennsylvania

We often explicitly regularize models, adding constraints that can improve generalization. We also implicitly regularize through algorithmic and architectural choices, such as early stopping or dropout. Theory can characterize these effects in simplified settings, but complex networks raise the need for empirical estimation and validation.

Our task is to find a mathematically expressible regularizer such that explicitly optimizing the empirical loss augmented by that regularizer mimics an implicitly biased procedure optimizing the loss alone. Given a parametric family of regularizers, we seek to estimate its parameters.

02 — ESTIMATING THE PENALTY

Reverse-engineering the regularization.

In Figure 1, we fixed the regularization and found the weights which minimize the regularized objective function. We can exploit the structure we saw above to reverse-engineer the regularization for a trained model: fix the learned weights θ^ and estimate the penalty parameters Λ.

A hypothetical minimum.

We often obtain weights that deviate from the strict loss minimum—for example, because we stop training before the loss has fully converged. Our key assumption is to characterize those learned weights as a minimizer of a hypothetical objective combining the empirical loss and a regularizer R(θ;Λ). The regularizer need not have been explicitly used during training.

Computing and matching gradients.

For differentiable objectives, stationarity requires the regularizer gradient to cancel the loss gradient. Both derivatives are taken with respect to the model weights θ and evaluated at the observed weights θ^:

∇θL(θ^)+∇θR(θ^;Λ)=0

Learning the regularizer. We fit a regularizer model R(θ;Λ) in which the predictive model’s weights θ^ are fixed and the regularization parameters Λ are learnable. When exact gradient matching is not possible, we minimize the mean squared error of this matching task. The training data stay fixed, and p is the number of weights.

Λ^=arg minΛ1p‖∇θL(θ^)+∇θR(θ^;Λ)‖22

The inverse calculation and its limits

In Figure 2, L(θ)=12(θ−2)2 and R(θ;λ)=λθ2 with λ≥0. Their derivatives give the residual (θ^−2)+2λθ^. For θ^≠0, minimizing its square has the closed-form solution:

λ^=max(0,2−θ^2θ^)

“Estimate λ” animates from the current guess to this exact least-squares solution; it is not a simulation of an iterative optimizer.

For 0<θ^≤2, the gradients can cancel exactly. Outside that interval, nonnegative ridge cannot explain the observed weight as an optimum: its best fit leaves a residual. At θ^=0, every λ supplies the same zero penalty gradient, so λ is unidentifiable. A fitted coefficient is an explanation within the chosen family, not evidence that this mechanism generated the weights.

Estimating regularization from training updates

An alternative perspective examines deviations along the training trajectory. At each step, architectural and algorithmic choices can make weight updates deviate from a reference path based on the loss gradient. These deviations provide another set of equations for estimating the regularization parameters, without assuming that the weights are at a minimum.

For the reference update −η∇L(θt), rearranging Δθt≈−η[∇L(θt)+∇R(θt;Λ)] gives:

∇θR(θt;Λ)≈−Δθtη−∇θL(θt)

Here Δθt=θt+1−θt and η is the step size. We can fit the same coefficient across several updates, or estimate a changing coefficient at each step. Setting Δθt≈0 recovers the endpoint-matching condition.

The reference must match the question. Comparing ordinary full-batch GD with its own update −η∇L gives zero discrepancy. To study the effect of finite GD steps, the paper instead compares a discrete step with continuous gradient flow over the same time interval. Figure 5 uses endpoint matching at different iterates; it does not fit these update discrepancies.

Stationarity and identifiability.

These quadratic bowls are convex, so a zero net gradient identifies the minimum. A neural network is more complicated: gradient cancellation alone establishes stationarity, not a global minimum. Several regularizers may explain the same endpoint.

With multiple penalty terms, we fit their coefficients together: −∇L≈λ1∇R1+λ2∇R2+⋯. Each weight contributes one equation. If two penalty gradients are nearly parallel, their separate coefficients can be difficult to identify even when their sum fits well.

04 — IMPLICIT REGULARIZATION

Estimating the regularization from stopping early.

Theory gives us a concrete example of implicit regularization: for ordinary least squares (OLS), gradient descent stopped after a finite number of steps from zero initialization has the same solution as an explicitly regularized regression. The equivalent penalty is quadratic, R(θ)=θ⊤Λ(t)θ, with a closed-form matrix Λ(t) determined by the data, step size, and stopping time. This matrix is the target for gradient matching.

We ask two different questions: can we recover that theoretical matrix, and can an estimated penalty recover the original model weights? A diagonal estimate from one endpoint can recover the weights while differing from theory. A full symmetric estimate uses weights from several independently trained models to recover the matrix. A single ridge coefficient provides a heuristic summary of how regularization decays over training.

The theoretical quadratic penalty and closed-form OLS solution

Let H=X⊤Xn=Vdiag(hi)V⊤ and use the loss L(θ)=‖Xθ−y‖22n. Full-batch gradient descent starts at zero with step size η satisfying 0<ηhi<1. At step t≥1, its weights minimize L(θ)+θ⊤Λ(t)θ, where

Λ(t)=12Vdiag[hi(1−ηhi)t1−(1−ηhi)t]V⊤

The eigenvectors give the penalty’s directions, and its eigenvalues decrease over training. The matrix is generally not diagonal in the original input coordinates. The factor ½ reflects the half-MSE loss convention used here; the paper writes its theoretical penalty with the loss ‖Xθ−y‖2/n.

For any estimated symmetric matrix Λ^, the explicit solution is θ^R=(H+2Λ^)−1X⊤yn, provided H+2Λ^ is positive definite. We solve this system independently to test weight recovery. The estimator receives weights and loss gradients, not the theoretical matrix or its eigenvectors.

Estimation methods and relation to the paper

These comparisons correspond to Figure 4 of the paper: full-matrix recovery in panel B, diagonal estimation in panel C, and a scalar ridge summary in panel E. The curvature ratio is the Hessian condition number: the largest curvature divided by the smallest.

We use 160 observations and 10 inputs; the paper uses 1000 observations. We construct X with Hessian eigenvalues ranging from 2 to 2/κ, use coefficient seed 7 and Gaussian noise with standard deviation 0.3, and run full-batch GD with η=0.2. Changing κ rebuilds the design. All endpoints use the same X and the same selected training step; only the true coefficients and noise are resampled.

Diagonal: fit ten signed coefficients in the original input coordinates, Λ^ii=−∂iL2θ^i. These are not coefficients in the Hessian eigenbasis. A zero weight makes its coefficient unidentifiable. Even with an exact gradient match, a unique minimum requires H+2Λ^ to be positive definite; the display checks this condition.

Full symmetric: fit all 55 independent entries by minimizing ∑k‖2Λ^θ^k+∇Lk‖2 over symmetric matrices. If the system is underdetermined, we select the solution with smallest Frobenius norm. Ten linearly independent endpoints identify the matrix; fewer leave unobserved directions. No theoretical eigenvectors are supplied. The endpoints share a stopping time; the paper also studies estimation across different stopping times.

Scalar ridge: fit Λ^=λ^I, using λ^=max(0,−θ^⊤∇L2‖θ^‖2). The right plot always shows this separate scalar summary, so it has the same meaning under every matrix selection. Its decline need not imply that scalar ridge recovers the full matrix or the stopped weights.

The matrix heatmaps compare against the independent theoretical formula above. Random initialization changes the starting weights for every endpoint while keeping the data fixed; the zero-start theoretical comparison is hidden in that case.

EVALUATING ESTIMATED REGULARIZATION

Two modes of successful estimation.

Gradient matching gives us computable equations whose unknowns are the parameters of a proposed regularizer. Once we fit those parameters, how can we decide whether the estimate is correct, or even a good approximation? We distinguish two modes of success:

  1. Recovery of hypothesized regularization. When the regularizer is known or predicted by theory, compare its estimated parameters with the known values. Figures 4 and 5 test this form of success for an explicit penalty and the theoretical early-stopping matrix.
  2. Recovery of the original model weights: validation via explicit regularization. Remove the training feature of interest, replace it with the estimated explicit penalty, and compare the resulting model with the original. Figure 5 demonstrates this for early stopping. Reproducing the weights need not uniquely identify the regularizer that produced them.

Our approach is an instance of parametric statistical modeling, with familiar limitations. The number and diversity of gradient equations constrain how many regularization parameters we can estimate. Collinear regularizer gradients make separate coefficients difficult to identify. A high gradient-fit score is useful evidence about the fit at the observed weights, but does not by itself establish recovery of the original model.

Additional dropout experiments in the paper illustrate both the usefulness and the limits of a heuristic regularizer model. Fitting scalar ℓ2 penalties reveals variation in the ridge-aligned component across dropout rates and architectures. However, these penalties explain only a small fraction of the loss gradient, and replacing dropout with them does not reproduce the original test performance.

The broader aim is to empirically validate theoretical results and study implicit regularization in complex networks that evade mathematical analysis. Even heuristic estimates can provide a diagnostic of how training configurations change the dynamics of learning. This could complement existing measures such as validation loss and weight or gradient norms; it is a starting point for characterizing implicit regularization, rather than a complete account of generalization.

The small examples presented here provide proof of concept. Extending this approach to larger models remains future work: it requires choosing informative candidate regularizers, isolating the effects of interacting training mechanisms through controlled ablations, and managing the cost of computing gradients and obtaining additional training endpoints. Each new architecture or training technique could be a substantial study in its own right.
