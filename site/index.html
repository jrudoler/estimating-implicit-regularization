Estimating implicit regularization in deep learning

Information about implicit regularization is embedded in the dynamics of learning. We use gradients of the loss and a proposed regularizer to estimate its unknown parameters.

Joseph H. Rudoler · Kevin Tan · Giles Hooker · Konrad P. Kording

We often explicitly regularize models, adding constraints that can improve generalization. We also implicitly regularize through algorithmic and architectural choices, such as early stopping or dropout. Theory can characterize these effects in simplified settings, but complex networks raise the need for empirical estimation and validation.

Our task is to find a mathematically expressible regularizer such that explicitly optimizing the empirical loss augmented by that regularizer mimics an implicitly biased procedure optimizing the loss alone. Given a parametric family of regularizers, we seek to estimate its parameters.

02 — ESTIMATING THE PENALTY

Reverse-engineering the regularization.

In Figure 1, we fixed the penalty and found the weights. We can exploit this structure to reverse-engineer the regularization: fix the learned weights θ̂ and estimate the penalty parameters Λ.

A hypothetical minimum.

We often obtain weights that deviate from the strict loss minimum—for example, because we stop training before the loss has fully converged. Our key assumption is to characterize those learned weights as a minimizer of a hypothetical objective combining the empirical loss and a regularizer R(θ; Λ). The regularizer need not have been explicitly used during training.

Computing and matching gradients.

For differentiable objectives, stationarity requires the regularizer gradient to cancel the loss gradient. Both derivatives are taken with respect to the model weights θ and evaluated at the observed weights θ̂:

∇θL(θ^)+∇θR(θ^;Λ)=0

Learning the regularizer. We fit a regularizer model R(θ; Λ) in which the predictive model’s weights θ̂ are fixed and the regularization parameters Λ are learnable. When exact gradient matching is not possible, we minimize the mean squared error of this matching task. The training data also stay fixed; p is the number of weights. We suppress the data argument in the notation.

Λ^=arg minΛ1p‖∇θL(θ^)+∇θR(θ^;Λ)‖22

In the scalar example below, L(θ) = ½(θ − 2)² and R(θ; λ) = λθ² with λ ≥ 0. Their derivatives give the residual (θ̂ − 2) + 2λθ̂. For θ̂ ≠ 0, minimizing its square has the closed-form solution:

λ^=max(0,2−θ^2θ^)

Select “Estimate λ” to see the total minimum move toward the fixed observed weight. The loss gradient stays constant while the penalty gradient changes. The extended weight range includes observations that no nonnegative L₂ penalty can explain; at θ̂ = 0, the penalty gradient is zero for every λ, so its strength cannot be identified.

The inverse calculation and its limits

“Estimate λ” animates from the current guess to this exact least-squares solution; it is not a simulation of an iterative optimizer.

For 0 < θ̂ ≤ 2, the gradients can cancel exactly. Outside that interval, nonnegative ridge cannot explain the observed weight as an optimum: its best fit leaves a residual. At θ̂ = 0, every λ supplies the same zero penalty gradient, so λ is unidentifiable. A fitted coefficient is an explanation within the chosen family, not evidence that this mechanism generated the weights.

Estimating regularization from training updates

An alternative perspective examines deviations along the training trajectory. At each step, architectural and algorithmic choices can make weight updates deviate from a reference path based on the loss gradient. These deviations provide another set of equations for estimating the regularization parameters, without assuming that the weights are at a minimum.

For the reference update −η∇L(θₜ), rearranging Δθₜ ≈ −η[∇L(θₜ) + ∇R(θₜ; Λ)] gives:

∇θR(θt;Λ)≈−Δθtη−∇θL(θt)

Here Δθₜ = θₜ₊₁ − θₜ and η is the step size. We can fit the same coefficient across several updates, or estimate a changing coefficient at each step. Setting Δθₜ ≈ 0 recovers the endpoint-matching condition.

The reference must match the question. Comparing ordinary full-batch GD with its own update −η∇L gives zero discrepancy. To study the effect of finite GD steps, the paper instead compares a discrete step with continuous gradient flow over the same time interval. Figure 5 uses endpoint matching at different iterates; it does not fit these update discrepancies.

Stationarity and identifiability.

These quadratic bowls are convex, so a zero net gradient identifies the minimum. A neural network is more complicated: gradient cancellation alone establishes stationarity, not a global minimum. Several regularizers may explain the same endpoint.

With multiple penalty terms, we fit their coefficients together: −∇L ≈ λ₁∇R₁ + λ₂∇R₂ + … . Each weight contributes one equation. If two penalty gradients are nearly parallel, their separate coefficients can be difficult to identify even when their sum fits well.

03 — RECOVERING EXPLICIT REGULARIZATION

Recovering an L₂ penalty in a neural network.

Before estimating implicit effects, we test whether the method recovers known explicit regularization. Train a small network on noisy samples of a curve, using tanh by default or ReLU for piecewise-linear predictions. We train with an explicit L₂ penalty and withhold its coefficient from the estimator. The estimate uses only the weights and the gradient of the unregularized training loss. During training, this endpoint estimate may be inaccurate because the gradients have not yet reached a balance.

This is a simplified version of the paper’s explicit elastic-net recovery experiment. Here we estimate one L₂ coefficient in a small, single-input network. The paper jointly estimates smoothed L₁ and L₂ coefficients in a 200-unit ReLU network with 10 inputs, across repeated datasets and penalty settings.

λ̂ = max(0, −θᵀ∇L / (2‖θ‖²))

This is a nonnegative least-squares projection of −∇L onto 2θ. “Gradient explained” is 1 − ‖∇L + 2λ̂θ‖² / ‖∇L‖². It is a fit diagnostic, not predictive R². Near-zero weights make the coefficient unidentifiable; a near-zero loss gradient makes the explained fraction uninformative. We display a dash for unavailable diagnostics.

For ReLU, we use a zero derivative at a kink. Tanh uses its smooth derivative, 1 − tanh²(z). Finite training time and nonsmoothness can leave a residual even when the loss appears stable. This experiment demonstrates explicit-penalty recovery, not proof that an arbitrary implicit bias is ridge regularization.

04 — IMPLICIT REGULARIZATION

Estimating the regularization from stopping early.

Theory gives us a concrete example of implicit regularization: for ordinary least squares (OLS), gradient descent stopped after a finite number of steps from zero initialization has the same solution as an explicitly regularized regression. The equivalent penalty is quadratic, R(θ) = θᵀΛ⁽ᵗ⁾θ, with a closed-form matrix Λ⁽ᵗ⁾ determined by the data, step size, and stopping time. This matrix is the target for gradient matching.

Following Figure 4 of the paper, we ask two different questions: can we recover that theoretical matrix, and can an estimated penalty recover the original model weights? A diagonal estimate from one endpoint can recover the weights while differing from theory (panel C). A full symmetric estimate uses multiple endpoints to recover the matrix (panel B). A single ridge coefficient provides a heuristic summary of how regularization decays over training (panel E).

The theoretical quadratic penalty and closed-form OLS solution

Let H = XᵀX/n = V diag(hᵢ)Vᵀ and use the loss L(θ) = ‖Xθ − y‖²/(2n). Full-batch gradient descent starts at zero with step size η satisfying 0 < ηhᵢ < 1. At step t ≥ 1, its weights minimize L(θ) + θᵀΛ⁽ᵗ⁾θ, where

Λ⁽ᵗ⁾ = ½ V diag[hᵢ(1 − ηhᵢ)ᵗ / (1 − (1 − ηhᵢ)ᵗ)] Vᵀ.

The eigenvectors give the penalty’s directions, and its eigenvalues decrease over training. The matrix is generally not diagonal in the original input coordinates. The factor ½ reflects the half-MSE loss convention used here; the paper writes its theoretical penalty with the loss ‖Xθ − y‖²/n.

For any estimated symmetric matrix Λ̂, the explicit solution is θ̂ᴿ = (H + 2Λ̂)⁻¹Xᵀy/n, provided H + 2Λ̂ is positive definite. We solve this system independently to test weight recovery. The estimator receives weights and loss gradients, not the theoretical matrix or its eigenvectors.

EVALUATING ESTIMATED REGULARIZATION

Two modes of successful estimation.

Gradient matching gives us computable equations whose unknowns are the parameters of a proposed regularizer. Once we fit those parameters, how can we decide whether the estimate is correct, or even a good approximation? The paper distinguishes two modes of success:

  1. Recovery of hypothesized regularization. When the regularizer is known or predicted by theory, compare its estimated parameters with the known values. The explicit-penalty experiment and the paper’s recovery of theoretically predicted implicit regularization test this form of success.
  2. Recovery of the original model weights: validation via explicit regularization. Remove the training feature of interest, replace it with the estimated explicit penalty, and compare the resulting model with the original. Figure 5 demonstrates this for early stopping. Reproducing the weights need not uniquely identify the regularizer that produced them.

Our approach is an instance of parametric statistical modeling, with familiar limitations. The number and diversity of gradient equations constrain how many regularization parameters we can estimate. Collinear regularizer gradients make separate coefficients difficult to identify. A high gradient-fit score is useful evidence about the fit at the observed weights, but does not by itself establish recovery of the original model.

The dropout experiments illustrate both the usefulness and the limits of a heuristic regularizer model. Fitting scalar L₂ penalties reveals variation in the ridge-aligned component across dropout rates and architectures. However, these penalties explain only a small fraction of the loss gradient, and replacing dropout with them does not reproduce the original test performance.

The broader aim is to empirically validate theoretical results and study implicit regularization in complex networks that evade mathematical analysis. Even heuristic estimates can provide a diagnostic of how training configurations change the dynamics of learning. This could complement existing measures such as validation loss and weight or gradient norms; it is a starting point for characterizing implicit regularization, rather than a complete account of generalization.
